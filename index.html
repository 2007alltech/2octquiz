Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi

1869 – 1948

Known as Bapu and later given the title Father of the Nation, Gandhi led India's freedom struggle through satyagraha — resistance to injustice through truth and non-violence rather than force.

Born2 Oct 1869, Porbandar, Gujarat
ParentsKaramchand & Putlibai Gandhi
WifeKasturba Gandhi (m. age 13)
Died30 Jan 1948, New Delhi

Early life & South Africa (1869–1915)

  • 1888: Sailed to London at 18 to study law at the Inner Temple; called to the Bar in 1891.
  • 1893: Travelled to South Africa for a legal case with the firm Dada Abdulla & Co. Was thrown off a first-class train carriage at Pietermaritzburg for refusing to move to third class — a turning point in his thinking on injustice.
  • 1894: Founded the Natal Indian Congress to organise Indians against discriminatory laws.
  • 1904: Established the Phoenix Settlement near Durban, and started the newspaper Indian Opinion.
  • 1906: First used the term and method of satyagraha (truth-force) to resist the Transvaal Asiatic Registration Act.
  • 1910: Set up Tolstoy Farm as a cooperative home for satyagrahis and their families.
  • 1915: Returned to India after roughly 21 years abroad, guided in Indian politics by his mentor Gopal Krishna Gokhale.

Leading the freedom movement in India (1915–1942)

  • 1917: Founded Sabarmati Ashram near Ahmedabad; led the Champaran Satyagraha in Bihar, his first in India, on behalf of indigo farmers forced to grow the crop under the exploitative tinkathia system.
  • 1918: Led the Kheda Satyagraha in Gujarat for tax relief after crop failure, and supported Ahmedabad's mill workers in a labour dispute, undertaking his first hunger strike.
  • 1919–1924: Backed the Khilafat Movement, building Hindu-Muslim unity against British policy, in the shadow of the 1919 Jallianwala Bagh massacre.
  • 1920–1922: Led the Non-Cooperation Movement, urging Indians to boycott British schools, courts, titles and foreign cloth. Called it off after the Chauri Chaura incident (Feb 1922), where a mob killed policemen.
  • 1930: Led the Dandi March (Salt March) — about 390 km on foot over 24 days from Sabarmati Ashram to the coastal village of Dandi — to make salt in defiance of the British salt tax.
  • 1931: Attended the Second Round Table Conference in London as the sole Congress representative.
  • 1932: Signed the Poona Pact with B. R. Ambedkar after a fast against separate electorates for the "depressed classes," whom he called Harijans.
  • 1940–41: Led a limited Individual Satyagraha, with Vinoba Bhave chosen as the first satyagrahi.
  • 1942: Launched the Quit India Movement on 8 August with the call "Do or Die," demanding an immediate end to British rule. Was jailed at the Aga Khan Palace, Pune, where his wife Kasturba died in 1944.

Final years, philosophy & death

  • 1944: Held talks with Muhammad Ali Jinnah on Hindu-Muslim unity as the demand for Pakistan grew.
  • 1947: Opposed but ultimately accepted the Partition of India; worked to calm communal violence in Calcutta and Delhi through fasts and prayer meetings.
  • 30 January 1948: Shot three times and killed at a prayer meeting at Birla House, New Delhi, by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist opposed to his conciliatory stance toward Muslims.
  • Philosophy: Satyagraha (truth-force), Ahimsa (non-violence), Swaraj (self-rule), Swadeshi (economic self-reliance, symbolised by hand-spun khadi cloth), and Sarvodaya (welfare of all).
  • Writings: Hind Swaraj (1909) and his autobiography, The Story of My Experiments with Truth; also edited Young India, Navajivan and Harijan.
  • Recognition: Given the title "Mahatma" (Great Soul) by Rabindranath Tagore; nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize five times but never awarded it. 2 October is marked worldwide as the International Day of Non-Violence.
Legacy: Gandhi's insistence that means matter as much as ends shaped India's path to independence in 1947 and influenced later civil rights leaders worldwide, including Martin Luther King Jr.

Lal Bahadur Shastri

1904 – 1966

India's second Prime Minister, Shastri is remembered for quiet, principled leadership — a man of modest means who held the country steady through war and hardship.

Born2 Oct 1904, Mughalsarai, UP
ParentsSharada Prasad Srivastava & Ramdulari Devi
WifeLalita Devi (m. 1928)
Died11 Jan 1966, Tashkent, USSR

Early life

  • Born to a schoolteacher-turned-clerk father who died when Shastri was still an infant; raised by his mother and maternal grandfather.
  • Studied at Harish Chandra High School, Varanasi, then at the nationalist Kashi Vidyapith, which awarded him the title "Shastri" (meaning "scholar") in the mid-1920s — a title, not a family name.
  • While working for the upliftment of Harijans at Muzaffarpur, he deliberately dropped his caste-derived surname, Srivastava.
  • Inspired at 16 by Gandhi and Madan Mohan Malaviya's call to join the Non-Cooperation Movement in 1921, leaving school to take part.

Freedom movement & early political career

  • Became president of the Servants of the People Society, founded by Lala Lajpat Rai, and joined the Indian National Congress in 1928.
  • Took part in the 1930 Salt Satyagraha, the Individual Satyagraha, and the 1942 Quit India Movement, spending a total of about nine years in British jails across these movements.
  • After independence, served in the Uttar Pradesh government as Minister of Police and Transport, introducing measures such as using water jets instead of lathi-charges to control crowds, and appointing the state's first women bus conductors.
  • Served as General Secretary of the All India Congress Committee, helping organise the party's campaign for the 1952 general elections.

Union minister under Nehru (1951–1964)

  • Railway Minister (1951–1956): After the September 1956 Mahbubnagar train accident, he offered his resignation, which Nehru refused. Following the Ariyalur train accident of 23 November 1956, he offered it again — this time accepting moral and constitutional responsibility though not personally at fault — and Nehru accepted it on 7 December 1956, calling it an example of "constitutional propriety."
  • 1957: Returned to the cabinet as Minister without Portfolio, later holding Transport & Communications and Commerce & Industry.
  • Home Minister (1961–1963): India's sixth Home Minister, before Nehru brought him back as Minister without Portfolio shortly before his own death.

Prime Minister (1964–1966)

  • Became Prime Minister on 9 June 1964, following Jawaharlal Nehru's death on 27 May 1964, at a time of economic strain and food shortages.
  • Promoted the White Revolution, backing Verghese Kurien's Amul milk cooperative model and the creation of the National Dairy Development Board.
  • Laid the groundwork for the Green Revolution, supporting high-yield seed varieties with Agriculture Minister C. Subramaniam to push India toward food self-sufficiency.
  • 1965 Indo-Pakistani War: Led India through the August–September 1965 war with Pakistan, giving the country its enduring slogan, "Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan" ("Hail the Soldier, Hail the Farmer"), honouring both defenders and food producers. He also asked citizens to skip an evening meal on Mondays to conserve foodgrain.
  • 10 January 1966, Tashkent: Signed the Tashkent Agreement with Pakistan's President Ayub Khan, mediated by Soviet Premier Alexei Kosygin, to formally end the war.
  • 11 January 1966: Died in Tashkent, hours after signing the agreement; the official cause recorded was a heart attack.
  • Honour: Awarded the Bharat Ratna, India's highest civilian award, posthumously in 1966 — the first Prime Minister to receive it after death.
  • Memorial: Cremated on the banks of the Yamuna in Delhi at a site now known as Vijay Ghat.
Legacy: Shastri is remembered for personal simplicity, integrity in office — most famously his 1956 resignation — and for steering India through one of its most difficult years without abandoning either its farmers or its soldiers.
